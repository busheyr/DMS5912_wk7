Section 1: Rhabdomyolysis & Acute Compartment Syndrome

Crush injury, prolonged immobilization, exertion and impaired muscle perfusion can destroy skeletal muscle cells. Rhabdomyolysis releases myoglobin, creatine kinase (CK), potassium, phosphate and other intracellular contents into circulation. The immediate priorities are recognition of life-threatening hyperkalemia, prevention of acute kidney injury (AKI), and identification of associated compartment syndrome.

1.1 How Damaged Muscle Threatens Kidneys and Heart

Myoglobin-associated AKI: Filtered myoglobin can promote pigment casts, tubular obstruction and oxidative injury, particularly during hypovolemia or acidic tubular conditions. It is more than simple “plug formation.”

Potassium and phosphate release: Cell destruction may cause rapid hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia and calcium disturbances. Obtain an ECG and repeat metabolic panels when the injury is significant.

Compartment syndrome: Disproportionate pain, pain with passive stretch, sensory change and a tense compartment raise concern. Pulses may remain palpable; urgent surgical assessment and fasciotomy when indicated are definitive—not a fluid-only treatment.

Monitor trends: Follow CK, creatinine, K+, bicarbonate, calcium, phosphate, urine output and clinical perfusion; no single CK value determines all interventions.

1.2 Treatment: Fluids First, Not Routine Urine Alkalinization

The core intervention is early, carefully titrated isotonic crystalloid. In adults with clinically significant rhabdomyolysis, either lactated Ringer’s or isotonic saline can be appropriate. The optimal rate is patient-specific, especially when there is oliguria, heart failure or pulmonary injury.

1.3 Airway Pharmacology: Succinylcholine Risk

Mechanism:

After major burns, extensive muscle trauma or denervation, extrajunctional acetylcholine receptors increase, creating the possibility of life-threatening potassium release with succinylcholine.

Timing caveat: This risk develops after the acute phase—commonly within the first 24–72 hours—and may persist for a prolonged period; the precise onset varies. Do not treat “older than 24 hours” as an absolute universal boundary.

Safer strategy: For affected patients at risk of hyperkalemia, a non-depolarizing paralytic such as rocuronium is generally preferred for rapid-sequence intubation; evaluate the full airway and physiologic context.

Section 2: Burn Resuscitation Formulas & Topical Burn Care

Major partial- and full-thickness burns produce capillary leak, inflammatory fluid loss and burn shock. Formal IV resuscitation is commonly needed in adults with at least 20% total body surface area (TBSA) burned; superficial erythema is excluded from TBSA calculations. Formulas estimate only the initial volume. Hourly physiologic response must guide subsequent adjustments.

2.1 Parkland Versus Modified Brooke

Formula / role

Modified Brooke / ABA-favored adult start

24-hour starting estimate (LR)

2 mL × body weight (kg) × %TBSA burn

Classic timing

Traditionally 50% within 8 h from burn time; 50% over next 16 h.

Most important limitation

ABA 2024 recommends starting adult ≥20% TBSA burns at 2 mL/kg/%TBSA to reduce excessive fluid exposure; adjust to response.

Parkland / historical comparator

24-hour starting estimate (LR)

4 mL × body weight (kg) × %TBSA burn

Classic timing

Traditionally 50% within 8 h from burn time; 50% over next 16 h.

Most important limitation

Useful to understand historic practice but can promote “fluid creep” if followed without reassessment.

Time zero: The traditional 8-hour period is measured from the moment of injury—not the time the patient reaches the hospital.

Endpoint in adults: Urine output commonly approximately 0.5 mL/kg/h (often 30–50 mL/h), along with mentation, perfusion, hemodynamics and other clinical indicators.

Children: Use pediatric/burn-center protocols, typically including maintenance fluid with dextrose when appropriate. Pediatric urine output targets vary with age and weight.

Over-resuscitation: Excess fluid can worsen pulmonary edema, extremity or abdominal compartment syndromes, and tissue swelling. Consult a burn center early.

2.2 Topical Burn Antimicrobials

Agent

Silver sulfadiazine (Silvadene)

Mechanism / clinical niche

Broad topical antimicrobial activity; sometimes used in selected deeper wounds under burn-team direction.

Key precautions

May delay healing of superficial partial-thickness burns; possible leukopenia; avoid near eyes and in susceptible patients such as neonates and those with relevant sulfonamide hypersensitivity.

Mafenide acetate (Sulfamylon)

Mechanism / clinical niche

Penetrates eschar and may be used for deep burns or exposed ear cartilage.

Key precautions

Application can be painful; carbonic anhydrase inhibition may contribute to metabolic acidosis.

Bacitracin / petrolatum

Mechanism / clinical niche

Moist wound care and limited topical antibacterial coverage for minor superficial partial-thickness burns, including selected facial burns.

Key precautions

Use clean dressings and local wound protocols; watch for contact allergy and avoid unnecessary prolonged topical antibiotics.

2.3 Chemical and Electrical Burns: Distinct Hazards

Chemical exposure: Protect rescuers, remove contaminated clothing, brush off dry powder before wet irrigation, then irrigate immediately and copiously. Irrigation duration depends on chemical and exposure; reassess tissue/ocular pH where indicated.

Hydrofluoric acid: After irrigation, urgent calcium gluconate treatment (commonly topical gel) binds fluoride; serious exposure can cause hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia and dangerous dysrhythmias. Severe cases require specialist-directed calcium therapy and ECG/electrolyte monitoring.

Electrical injury:

Skin findings can grossly underestimate deep muscle damage, compartment syndrome and rhabdomyolysis. Obtain an ECG, assess trauma and monitor K+, CK, renal function and urine output.

Electrical fluid endpoint:

Higher targets—often about 1 mL/kg/h or 75–100 mL/h in adults with myoglobinuria—may be used under specialized protocols, rather than automatically applying the standard thermal-burn formula.

Section 3: Local Anesthetic Systemic Toxicity (LAST)

Bupivacaine, lidocaine and ropivacaine block voltage-gated sodium channels to stop nerve conduction. Inadvertent intravascular injection or excessive systemic absorption can disrupt brain and cardiac conduction. LAST is a time-critical resuscitation emergency that may present with neurologic signs, cardiovascular collapse or both.

3.1 Recognition: Early Neurologic and Cardiovascular Findings

Neurologic warning signs: Metallic taste, perioral/tongue numbness, tinnitus, dizziness and agitation may precede generalized seizures or CNS depression.

Cardiac toxicity: Bradycardia, widening QRS, ventricular dysrhythmias and cardiovascular collapse are possible; bupivacaine is especially cardiotoxic.

Atypical or delayed onset: The classic symptom sequence is not universal; cardiac arrest may be the first sign, and symptoms may occur after an observation delay. Do not wait for a seizure before acting.

3.2 20% Lipid Emulsion Rescue and Modified Resuscitation

Step

1. Stop drug / call for help

Faculty teaching action

Immediately stop local anesthetic; summon resuscitation team; obtain the LAST checklist and 20% lipid rescue kit.

Operational guardrail

Optimize airway, oxygenation and ventilation; avoid hypoxemia and severe acidosis.

2. Treat seizures

Faculty teaching action

Benzodiazepines are preferred (e.g., midazolam or lorazepam).

Operational guardrail

Avoid large propofol doses in hemodynamically unstable patients; propofol is not a lipid-rescue substitute.

3. Give lipid 20%

Faculty teaching action

For <70 kg: ~1.5 mL/kg IV bolus over 2–3 min, then ~0.25 mL/kg/min. For >70 kg: ASRA uses ~100 mL bolus then ~200–250 mL over 15–20 min.

Operational guardrail

Continue infusion ≥10 min after stable. If still unstable: repeat bolus and double rate per checklist; maximum cumulative lipid dose ~12 mL/kg.

4. Modified ACLS

Faculty teaching action

If epinephrine needed, give small boluses ≤1 mcg/kg. Amiodarone is favored for ventricular arrhythmias when appropriate.

Operational guardrail

Avoid vasopressin, beta blockers, calcium-channel blockers and lidocaine/other sodium-channel-blocking antiarrhythmics.

5. Observe / escalate

Faculty teaching action

Continue monitored care after stabilization; consult toxicology/anesthesia and consider cardiopulmonary bypass/ECMO for refractory collapse.

Operational guardrail

Prolonged CPR may be needed. Use the institution’s current ASRA-style checklist.

Match the operational guardrail to the rescue action

3.3 Why Lipid Rescue Works

Lipid shuttle / partitioning:

The lipid emulsion helps redistribute lipophilic local anesthetic away from vulnerable cardiac and neural tissues and can support myocardial metabolism.

Practical response:

Early recognition, oxygenation, seizure control, appropriate 20% lipid dosing and a modified resuscitation approach work together—lipid alone is not a replacement for ACLS or expert support.

Reference Notes

Clinical teaching updates reviewed November 2026. The supplied module outline is retained in sequence and topic coverage, with the following evidence-based clarifications: rhabdomyolysis fluids are titrated to urine output instead of a universal 500–1,000 mL/h order; bicarbonate is not recommended for routine AKI prevention; the 2024 American Burn Association guideline favors initiating adult major-burn resuscitation at 2 mL/kg/%TBSA; succinylcholine risk is dependent on injury phase rather than an exact 24-hour cutoff; and the ASRA LAST checklist includes a lipid maximum of about 12 mL/kg and repeat-bolus guidance. These are instructional principles, not stand-alone patient orders. Verify all medication doses and local protocols before clinical use.
